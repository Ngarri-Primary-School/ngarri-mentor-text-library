# The Boy Who Loved Words - complete revision draft

Status: `teacher_reviewed` - approved by Phill on 18 September 2026; published to Supabase and the shared progress viewer

## Book details

Author: Roni Schotter
Illustrator: Giselle Potter
Suggested year band: Years 2-6, with selected read-aloud work suitable for Year 1.
Text type: Narrative picture book.
Genres: Literary fantasy; vocabulary-rich quest narrative; bildungsroman.

Rationale: Selig's imagined journey gives narrative shape to a question about language and belonging. The story follows a collector who is mocked for his difference, leaves home to find a purpose, discovers that words gain power when shared, and returns with a changed understanding of himself.

## Why use this book?

- A memorable mentor text for choosing, testing and sharing precise vocabulary for a purpose.
- Shows that a word changes meaning and effect with its context: words describe, delight, repair, sell and connect people.
- Supports close reading of sound pattern, alliteration, imagery and playful invented situations.
- Makes a strong inferential study of Selig's loneliness, belonging and changing identity.
- Opens a generous discussion about passions, difference, creativity and how language affects a community.

## Writing connections

### Word Choice - Year 2

Key Understanding: "word choices enhance the reader's experience"

Key Skill: "I can identify and use memorable words and phrases."

In this book: Selig's collecting is built through memorable sound-rich groups such as "tintinnabulating", "tantalizing" and "stirring", then through the poet's "lemon lozenge" and "licorice sky". The words are not decoration: their repeated sounds make readers hear why Selig values them. Students can identify the word or phrase that does the most work in a sentence, then choose one memorable word for a real object, sound or feeling rather than adding a random long word.

### Word Choice - Year 3

Key Understanding: "word choices are selected and refined, allowing the writer to communicate a message clearly"

Key Skill: "I can choose specific words and phrases for a desired effect."

In this book: Schotter contrasts the poet's rejected "macaroon" with the selected words "lozenge", "lemon" and "licorice". Each surviving word helps the moon image become precise in colour, shape and atmosphere. Later, the baker's words are chosen for particular food rather than scattered as a general list. Students can test alternatives against an intended effect, explaining why one word belongs and another attractive word does not.

### Word Choice - Year 4

Key Understanding: "word choice can help my reader visualise my ideas"

Key Skill: "I can use specific words to encourage my reader to visualise."

In this book: On his journey Selig notices the "nod and toddle" of tulips, the "sway and swagger" of branches and the arrival of "dusk". The verbs and paired sounds make movement and evening visible. Potter extends that choice through word slips placed among the tulips, branches and later the bakery goods. Students can revise a general setting sentence by selecting verbs that let a reader picture how things move.

### Audience/Voice - Year 3

Key Understanding: "Writing can capture a general mood and evoke emotions such as joy, fear, excitement and humour."

Key Skill: "I can create an identifiable tone such as compassionate, bittersweet, frustrated or terrified."

In this book: The narrator's affectionate, playful voice celebrates Selig's delight in language while allowing the loneliness beneath it to remain visible. The comic Genie scene and exaggerated vocabulary invite enjoyment; the classroom picture, the label "oddball" and the later single word "Solo" create a bittersweet counterweight. Students can track how a narrator can make a reader admire a character's passion without pretending that the character is never hurt.

### Organisation - Year 4

Key Understanding: "different text types have different structures to suit the purpose"

Key Skill: "I can develop storylines, characters and settings to create a cohesive narrative (narrative only)."

In this book: The opening establishes Selig's collecting and isolation; the dream gives him a mission; the tree and poet reveal that words can become useful when shared; the bakery and neighbours test that discovery; and the final encounter with Melody resolves the remaining loneliness. The repeated movement from collecting to scattering, and the recurring word slips in Potter's illustrations, hold these episodes together. Students can map how each encounter changes what Selig understands about his purpose.

## Reading connections

### Inferring - Year 1

Key Understanding: "Inferring is when we work out what the author doesn't exactly say."

Key Skill: "I can infer character feelings using text clues such as pictures and words."

In this book: Selig does not say that he is ashamed in the classroom scene. Readers combine the children giggling, the word "oddball" on the blackboard, his isolated desk and the narration that the word leaves him lonely. Students can distinguish the clue from the inference, then compare his response to "Wordsworth" at the poet's tree, where he claims the name proudly.

### Synthesising - Year 2

Key Understanding: "That synthesis is when we use our prior knowledge and the text to build deeper understandings. (Synthesising)"

Key Skill: "I can identify how the new knowledge developed my thinking. (synthesising)"

In this book: At first, Selig and many readers may think his goal is to collect the largest possible store of beautiful words. The heavy rucksack, the tree, the poet's successful line and the changed bakery revise that idea: words become most valuable when they help someone communicate. Students can record an initial idea about Selig's collection, identify the event that changes it, and explain their final understanding of his purpose.

### Inferring - Year 3

Key Understanding: "Using a range of text clues can help me make predictions and inferences."

Key Skill: "I can infer a character's traits using different clues."

In this book: Selig's generosity is shown before it is named. He leaves his words where a struggling poet can use them, chooses food words that make a baker's goods appealing, and uses words to interrupt neighbours' quarrel. His later loneliness matters because he has become helpful to many people without yet finding mutual companionship. Students can use words, actions and Potter's images of scattered slips to infer generosity, persistence and a continuing need for belonging.

### Analysing - Year 4

Key Understanding: "Different text types have typical stages and language features dependent on their purpose."

Key Skill: "I can explain how author's use different literary devices to add to the meaning of the text. (grammar, word choices, free verse, symbolism, personification, metaphor etc.)"

In this book: The word slips operate as a visual symbol of language becoming active in the world. Early slips clutter Selig's clothes and isolate him; at the tree they become possibilities; later they float into a poem, a bakery, a dispute and the final musical scene. Students can analyse how this repeated visual device changes from burden to connection, and why the story needs both words and pictures to make that change clear.

## PRIDE values

### Determination

Where to look: Selig leaves home after the Genie's challenge, continues when his word-filled load becomes too heavy, and keeps experimenting until he finds how words can serve others.

In this book: Selig is not determined simply because he travels. He changes his approach when collecting alone no longer works: he hangs the words safely in the tree, notices what the poet can do with them, and develops a new way to pursue his passion. This supports discussion of determination as learning from a setback and finding a new path, not stubbornly carrying the same burden.

## Inquiry lenses

### Identity, Creativity and Wellbeing

Where to look: Selig is called "Wordsworth" as a taunt, later chooses the name proudly, and learns that his unusual passion can become a creative contribution while still needing friendship.

In this book: The book resists a simple message that being talented automatically makes someone happy. Selig develops confidence by valuing his passion and using it well, but he also remains lonely until he finds Melody. Students can inquire into how people can honour a distinctive interest, contribute it to others and still need relationships where they are known and valued.

### Social Responsibility

Where to look: The poet finds language for a poem, the baker's customers understand the appeal of the food, and neighbours recognise their quarrel after Selig scatters particular words.

In this book: Selig's words affect a community in different ways: they enable expression, make communication more vivid and interrupt harmful interaction. Students can investigate the question: when does language help people understand, appreciate or care for one another, and when can it exclude or wound, as the children do with "oddball"?

## Structured teaching ideas

### Choose the word that belongs

Linked focus: Word Choice - Years 2-3.

Suggested years: Years 2-3.

Time/grouping/support: 30 minutes; whole class then pairs; moderate support.

Where to look: The poet selects "lozenge", "lemon" and "licorice" for the moon line and leaves "macaroon" behind.

Learning focus: Select vocabulary because it creates the intended image and sound, not because it is merely unusual.

Teaching sequence: Read the poet's choices aloud. Ask what each selected word contributes to colour, shape, sound or mood. Offer a tempting but mismatched alternative and model rejecting it with evidence. Jointly build a short description from a small bank of possible words, explaining each decision.

Student application: Students write a two-sentence description of a familiar object or place, test three possible word choices and annotate the one they kept with the intended effect.

Notice learning: Students justify a choice in relation to the image or mood and can identify why an alternative does not belong.

### Follow Selig's changing purpose

Linked focus: Synthesising; Organisation.

Suggested years: Years 2-4.

Time/grouping/support: 35 minutes; pairs then class discussion; moderate support.

Where to look: Selig's bulging clothes, heavy rucksack, tree, the poet's poem and the later community episodes.

Learning focus: Revise an interpretation as events reveal a character's changing purpose.

Teaching sequence: Begin with "What does Selig think words are for?" after the opening. Add an evidence card after each turning point. Model changing the first answer after the poet uses the words, rather than erasing it. End by sorting events into collecting, discovering and sharing.

Student application: Students create a before/during/after pathway explaining how Selig's purpose changes, using a short quotation or illustration detail at each point.

Notice learning: Students name the evidence that changed their thinking and distinguish collecting words from using words purposefully.

### Infer the feeling behind "oddball"

Linked focus: Inferring.

Suggested years: Years 1-3.

Time/grouping/support: 25 minutes; teacher modelling then partners; high support.

Where to look: The classroom picture, the children's laughter, the blackboard word and Selig's response.

Learning focus: Combine verbal and visual evidence to infer an unspoken feeling.

Teaching sequence: Cover the final sentence initially and model making an inference from one picture clue. Add the dialogue and narration, then revise the claim. Separate a clue card from an inference card so students can see that feelings are not guesses without evidence.

Student application: Partners complete: "Selig may feel ___ because the words show ___ and the illustration shows ___."

Notice learning: Students supply compatible evidence from both modes and avoid treating the word "oddball" alone as a complete explanation.

### Turn a word collection into a scene

Linked focus: Audience/Voice; Word Choice.

Suggested years: Years 3-5.

Time/grouping/support: 40 minutes; pairs then independent writing; moderate support.

Where to look: The bakery and neighbours' scenes, where word choices alter what people notice and do.

Learning focus: Use a small, purposeful set of words to create a tone and change a situation.

Teaching sequence: Compare the food words with the quarrel words. Ask how the sound and connotations position readers differently. Model planning a miniature scene around a need, then choosing only three or four words that serve it.

Student application: Students write a short scene in which a character uses carefully selected language to help, invite, calm or explain. They underline the words that create the desired tone.

Notice learning: Vocabulary supports a recognisable purpose and tone rather than appearing as a disconnected list.

### Read the word slips as a visual symbol

Linked focus: Analysing; Presentation.

Suggested years: Years 4-6.

Time/grouping/support: 35 minutes; small groups; moderate support.

Where to look: Selig's crowded clothing, the word tree, the poet's page, bakery, neighbours and final spread.

Learning focus: Explain how a recurring visual element changes meaning across a narrative.

Teaching sequence: Give groups still images in narrative order without the text. Ask what changes about the slips and how that change may represent Selig's relationship with language. Reveal the accompanying words and revise interpretations. Model distinguishing "the slips are everywhere" from an analysis of what their placement signifies.

Student application: Students create a visual motif plan for a narrative, showing it in three moments and annotating how its meaning changes.

Notice learning: Students explain the relationship between the image pattern, character change and narrative message.

### Words that build or damage belonging

Linked focus: Social Responsibility; Identity, Creativity and Wellbeing.

Suggested years: Years 3-6.

Time/grouping/support: 35 minutes; circle discussion then individual reflection; moderate support.

Where to look: The children naming Selig "Wordsworth" and "oddball", the poet's dedication, the neighbour scene and Selig's meeting with Melody.

Learning focus: Consider how language can acknowledge difference, exclude someone or create connection.

Teaching sequence: Sort the book's language moments into possible effects without labelling speakers as simply good or bad. Discuss why "Wordsworth" changes for Selig between the classroom and the tree. Co-construct distinctions between a name used to tease, a name claimed with pride and words used with care.

Student application: Students write a brief reflection or dialogue showing a word or name used in two different contexts, then explain how the relationship and intent change its effect.

Notice learning: Students recognise that a word's effect depends on context, relationship and intention, and can explain how the book complicates belonging.
